# Administration

The Administration Dashboard is the deployment's console. What anyone may do across the deployment comes from deployment roles (`app::deployment`, with the roles themselves in `app::deployment_role`, tables `deployment_role` and `user_deployment_role`), ranked by position like a community's and holding deployment permissions: `viewDashboard` (the totals, growth, fleet, directories, and moderation log), `manageRegistrationInvites`, `manageVoiceServers` (the `/voice-servers` registry), `manageDeploymentRoles`, `moderateCommunities` (below), `manageFederation` (the gates and the directory of other deployments; see Federation), `reviewReports` (reading report cases, dismissing them, and resolving them with a warning; see Reports), `removeContent` (deleting a reported message, clearing a reported nickname, resetting a reported profile, and deleting a banned person's recent messages, acting only on what a case or a ban names), `manageReportCategories` (see Reports), `banUsers` (bans from the deployment, below), `messageAnyUser` (a DM with anyone, past shared communities and blocks; see Threads and DMs), `manageDeploymentSettings` (the deployment's profile and policies, below, and deleting bots whose owners are gone; see Bots), and `managePlugins` (installed plugins' settings, mode, order, and whether they run; installing them is the terminal's; see Plugins). Each permission does something whole on its own, and each step of a task takes the permission for what that step does, so no role holds a power it cannot use. Where one is part of another, holding the greater gives the lesser (`DeploymentPermission::includes`: Moderate any community includes Remove content): the access computed for a user, `GET /users/@me/admin`, and `deploymentAccessChanged` all carry what is included, and `GET /users/@me/admin` lists the inclusions (`inclusions`) so the dashboard's role editor shows an included permission held and fixed. The user and community directories open to holders of View dashboard or of any moderation permission but Message any user (`DeploymentPermissions::DIRECTORIES`). A holder of Manage deployment roles creates, edits, reorders, deletes, gives, and takes only roles below their own highest, and gives only permissions they hold, a role to someone included (giving a role takes holding everything it allows; taking it away, rank alone), from the dashboard (`/admin/roles`, `/admin/role-order`, `/admin/users/{user}/roles/{role}`, `api::deployment`). Only the terminal reaches the top role: `aspen-chat-server admin grant <username>` gives it (making an Administrator role with every permission but moderation when there is none), `revoke` takes every role, `list` shows who holds what, and `admin allow|deny <permission>` changes what the top role allows (refusing to deny a permission that one it allows includes), which is how moderation is first granted (each announces the change to those it touches as the dashboard does, `deploymentAccessChanged`, and rechecks their calls, so the terminal needs NATS as the servers do): `admin grant`'s Administrator role holds every permission but `DeploymentPermissions::MODERATION` (Moderate any community, Review reports, Remove content, Ban users, and Message any user). Community roles never reach any of this, since a dashboard anyone could open would let anyone mint the invites an invite-only deployment is closed by. The API is `/admin/*` (`api::admin`, `app::admin`), every handler taking `AdminUser`, which refuses anyone without a deployment permission with `403` `adminRequired` and hands the handler what they may do, each requiring its own permission (`forbidden` without it); `GET /users/@me/admin` tells a client what the caller may do and which roles give it, and a change reaches them as the `deploymentAccessChanged` event.

A deployment role may have a hue (`hue`, 0 to 359 around HSV's wheel; `POST /admin/roles`, `PATCH /admin/roles/{role}`, where `null` takes it away), in which the names of its holders are drawn everywhere, over any community role's (see Roles and permissions). Each user's `nameHue`, on the user record everyone reads, is the hue of the highest deployment role they hold that has one (`user.name_hue`); every change that can move it, a hue edited, roles reordered or deleted, a role given or taken from the dashboard or the terminal, recomputes it for those it touches in the same transaction and announces each change as an update of the user to everyone who sees them (`app::deployment_role::refresh_name_hues`). Only the hue leaves the dashboard: which roles someone holds stays the dashboard's. A user of another deployment carries this deployment's hue here, never their home's.

Moderate any community is the deployment's power over what its users post (`app::permissions::MODERATION`): its holder reaches every community and DM without belonging, views every channel whatever the overrides, ranks above every role but the owner, and may delete messages, attachments, reactions, and poll write-ins, remove members (never the owner), rename and delete channels and communities, and list anyone's DMs (`GET /admin/users/{user}/dms`); nothing else, so a moderator posts nowhere they could not already, and joins and reshapes no DM: adding someone to a group DM, or leaving one, takes being in it. Each use that the community's own permissions would not have allowed, and every reading of a DM by someone not in it (`app::permissions::channel_access_reading`, the only way to read one; see Threads and DMs), is written to `moderation_log` (`app::moderation_log`) and the server's log, and the dashboard shows it (`GET /admin/moderation-log`) to those who may view it, each entry with what its ids name as the log is read (`details`, one query per kind of record for a page: the community, the channel or DM and its people, the message and its author, the person acted on, and the emoji, file name, write-in, or new name), so the dashboard names things rather than showing ids. A moderator's event stream follows the communities they belong to, where it shows every channel; elsewhere they read over REST, and the client applies their writes to its cache itself. The dashboard reads the record of files offered in calls (`/admin/file-transfers`, which takes Moderate any community; see File transfers), the deployment's totals (`/admin/overview`) and their growth (`/admin/growth?range=threeMonths|sixMonths|oneYear|fiveYears|allTime`: users and communities at each day up to half a year, week up to two years, and month beyond, each counted from creation to deletion, summed from one pass over both tables), its users and communities (searched by any part of a name through `filter[name]`, served by trigram indexes; ordered by `sort`, a field `-` prefixed for descending, newest first by default; paged by `offset` and `limit`, 15 by default, because a page must follow whatever column sorts it, which the keyset parameters the rest of the API uses cannot), registration invites, and the fleet. Powers too strong for even a privileged web API, such as suspending rate limits, stay terminal commands.

With the deployment setting `registration_invite_required` (below), `POST /users` takes `inviteCode` and refuses without one (`registrationInviteRequired`) or with one that is not usable (`registrationInviteInvalid`); `GET /auth/methods` says which, as `registrationInviteRequired`. A registration invite (`registration_invite`, `app::registration_invite`) may be used `max_uses` times, until `expires_at` if set, and not once revoked; it is redeemed under a row lock in the transaction that makes the account, so concurrent registrations cannot overdraw it, and the account records it (`user.registered_with`). Where invites are optional, a code is still recorded when it works and ignored when it does not. Administrators make and revoke invites at `/admin/registration-invites`, which lists every usable invite and, for a week after it stops working (`app::invite::STALE_AFTER_DAYS`), one that no longer does: stopped is the first of being revoked, being used up (`used_up_at`, set when the last use is taken), and expiring; the terminal's `invites list` shows them all. A community's invite list (`GET /communities/{community}/invites`) follows the same week for invites that have expired; a revoked community invite is gone at once; an invite-only deployment's first account needs one from the terminal (`aspen-chat-server invites create [--uses N] [--expires 7d] [--note …]`), after which its owner grants themselves administration.

The deployment's settings are the one row of `deployment_settings` (`app::deployment_settings`, `api::deployment_settings`), which a migration inserts and whose key, `singleton`, can hold nothing but true: how it presents itself, the policies its administrators set, and the federation gates (see Federation), each with its default in the column. `aspen.toml` keeps what a server needs to start and what is bound to its domain; what the deployment's administrators decide is here, so every API server follows one answer and a change needs no restart. The profile is a display name and an icon, which the sign-in screens welcome people with ("Welcome to {name}, an Aspen Chat instance.", or "Welcome to our Aspen Chat instance." without a name, under the icon, or Aspen's without one); the name, or "Aspen" without one (`DeploymentSettings::name`), is also what the system account is called (see Threads and DMs) and what authenticator apps and passkey prompts call the deployment (`otpauth` URIs and the relying party's name, each built as it is used). The icon goes to `NULL` when its icon row is deleted. `GET /deployment` answers the profile to anyone, signed in or not, with the icon as its record (`downloadUrl` included, since a signed-out client cannot read `/icons/{icon}`); `PATCH /deployment` changes it, and `GET` and `PATCH /admin/settings` read and change the policies: `registration_invite_required`, `require_two_factor` (see Sign-in and security), `bots_enabled` and `bots_max_per_user` (see Bots), `everyone_mention_limit` (see Roles and permissions), `custom_emoji_limit` (see Custom emoji), `file_transfers` (see File transfers), and `email_required`, `email_verification_required`, and `newsletter_enabled` (see Email; each turns on only where `[email]` is configured, which `emailAvailable` in the policies and `email` in the profile say); both take Manage deployment settings, and the gates are changed at `PATCH /admin/federation` with Manage federation (`update_as` checks each part of a change against its permission). A display name is trimmed and from 1 to 64 characters, in any script; an icon must be one whose upload is confirmed (`deploymentIconMissing` otherwise); a count is at most what `INTEGER` holds. The terminal changes any of them (`aspen-chat-server settings show|set`, `operator::settings`), which is how an invite-only deployment is made one before anyone has an account. `federation_domain` is the domain this deployment is known by, pinned the first time a server starts with an `https` `public_url`, whose host it is (`pin_domain`, which the terminal's `settings set` runs too, so gates can open before any server has run); a server started with another, or with none once it is pinned, refuses to start, since other deployments pin its key at that name, and a gate opens only where it is set (a check on the row backs that).

Every API server keeps a copy of the settings (`SettingsCache`, `GlobalServerContext::settings`), read as it starts, which requests read instead of the row. A change locks the row, checks the settings as it would leave them, writes them with `revision` one higher, renames the system account if the name changed, and, before committing, writes the revision to the NATS key-value bucket `aspen_settings`; each server watches the bucket (`spawn_watcher`) and reads the row until it holds that revision, every twenty milliseconds for up to ten seconds, after which the change must have rolled back and the row stands. A server that loses the watch reads the row afresh as it follows it again. The server that made a change takes its copy at once. Who can observe the settings: the profile, anyone, through `GET /deployment`; whether registering takes an invite and two factors are required, anyone, through `GET /auth/methods`; the policies and gates, holders of their permissions. No event carries them: the signed-out screens and the dashboard read them when they open. What is already open when one changes is brought in line by whoever made the change, once it commits (`after_change`): turning file transfers on or off rechecks every call (`Recheck::Everyone`), whose participants' grants then follow; a change to an immigration gate or its shared list signs out the users of other deployments the gates no longer admit and takes them out of their calls (`standing::shut_out`), as the standing check would; and each server's open event streams follow its copy, a stream whose account lacked a second factor when it identified closing with 4403 when the deployment starts requiring one (`api::event_stream`), so the client asks for one as it would at sign-in. Turning bots off leaves bots already made working, and a lower limit is not applied to what already exceeds it: bots owned, emoji held, or communities already past the everyone mention limit. CORS follows the gates as each request arrives. `scripts/check_permissions.py` checks file transfers reaching a call, two factors closing a stream from the terminal, and the permission; `scripts/dev_federation_check.py` checks a gate closed from the dashboard ending a visitor's session.

Fleet health (`app::fleet`) comes from heartbeats: every `HEARTBEAT_INTERVAL` (ten seconds) each API server, private workers included (which show no streams or requests), writes its uptime, open event streams, requests and server errors per minute, resident memory, and database connections to the NATS key-value bucket `aspen_fleet`, whose entries expire after thirty seconds, so a server that stops drops out. The figures are counted where the matching Prometheus metrics are recorded; the heartbeat is how the dashboard sees every server without reaching their loopback metrics endpoints. Voice servers' health is their registry rows: enabled, capacity, and whether their last report is within `offer_silence_seconds`.

A ban from the deployment (`app::user_ban`, `user.banned_at`, with `ban_reason` and `banned_until`) is how a deployment stops someone using it at all, its own users and visitors from elsewhere alike. `PUT /admin/users/{user}/ban` bans (`201`, or `200` replacing a ban that stood) with an optional `reason`, `durationSeconds`, and `deleteMessagesSeconds` (3600 or 86400: their messages anywhere on the deployment, DMs included, from the last hour or day go with the ban, which takes Remove content besides), and for a bot `withOwner` to ban its owner too; `DELETE` lifts. Both take Ban users and rank by deployment roles: only someone whose highest role is below the banner's, never the banner or the system account, is banned, has their ban replaced, or has it lifted, so a ban of someone who outranks the caller is not theirs to lift. A ban revokes every sign-in they hold, publishes `accountBanned` (its reason and end) to their own subject, after which the event feed closes each of their streams (`FeedEvent::ends`, close code 4410), and takes them out of every call (`app::voice::kick_everywhere`; a join not yet reported is removed as its report is applied). While it stands (`user_ban::BANNED_SQL`: made, and not yet ended), every sign-in is refused at `app::login::issue_session` with `deploymentBanned` and the reason and end in its detail (a password sign-in learns so before any second factor is asked for), any token still held resolves to no one (a bot's included, which keeps its tokens for when the ban is lifted), and other deployments asking after them are told `refused` (see Federation). They stay in their communities, so their name and what they posted still show. Each ban and lift is in the moderation log (`banUser`, `liftUserBan`). The user directory takes `filter[banned]=true` and gives each standing ban as `ban`; it is one of the directories every moderation permission opens.

A dual invite is a registration invite that names a community invite (`registration_invite.community_invite`): the account it makes joins that community in the same transaction (`app::registration_invite::join_invited`, through `app::community::add_member`, which announces the membership and checks community bans), and a foreign user's first arrival with it does the same (`app::federation::abroad`). Holders of Manage registration invites make one with `community` on `POST /admin/registration-invites`, from the dashboard or a community's invite dialog; the community invite is made by them (`app::invite::insert`), so it needs Create invites there like any invite (a non-member, or a moderator without it, is refused), expires with the registration invite, and is listed and announced among the community's invites to those who manage them. The dashboard's record names the community and whether its invite still works (`community`, read for a page in one query). `GET /registration-invites/{code}` (unauthenticated, limited per address against guessing) shows a usable invite to someone about to register, with the community invite and, sideloaded, the community, so the page can say which community the account joins and send someone already signed in to join it instead. Revoking the dual invite revokes its community invite in the same transaction (from the dashboard or `invites revoke`, which connects to NATS to announce it); revoking the community invite from the community leaves a registration invite whose accounts join nothing, and joining passes over a community invite that no longer works, or a deleted community, rather than refusing the account. What decides it is the registration invite's usability and the community invite's, both checked as the account is made; an account already made is an ordinary member, removed or banned like any. `scripts/check_permissions.py` checks making, joining, and both revocations.
